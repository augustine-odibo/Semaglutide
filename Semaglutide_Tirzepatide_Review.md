## Abstract

Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) are once-weekly incretin-based peptides that produce glucose lowering and weight loss approaching that achieved with bariatric surgery. Although both mimic gut-derived incretin hormones, they differ fundamentally in receptor pharmacology. Semaglutide is a selective, albumin-bound glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonist with a half-life of about one week. Tirzepatide is a single GIP-based peptide that engages the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR) more than GLP-1R and acts at GLP-1R as a cAMP-biased agonist with reduced receptor internalisation. Both drugs restore glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon, but most of their effect on body weight arises from reduced energy intake driven by GLP-1R signalling at circumventricular, hypothalamic and hindbrain sites; slowing of gastric emptying is largely lost with chronic dosing. The GIPR component of tirzepatide adds weight-independent insulin sensitisation through adipose tissue, central anorexigenic signalling and, in preclinical models, suppression of GLP-1R agonist-induced emesis. These differences are consistent with the superiority of tirzepatide over semaglutide for weight loss (20.2% vs 13.7% at 72 weeks in SURMOUNT-5). Outcome trials show that semaglutide reduces major adverse cardiovascular events and kidney disease progression, largely independently of weight loss, while tirzepatide reduces heart failure events in obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. We synthesise the molecular, physiological and clinical evidence for these mechanisms, examine the unresolved paradox that both GIPR agonism and antagonism enhance weight loss, discuss mechanism-linked adverse effects, and outline the multi-receptor and oral agents now emerging.

**Keywords:** semaglutide; tirzepatide; GLP-1 receptor; GIP receptor; incretin; obesity; type 2 diabetes; biased agonism

## 1. Introduction

Semaglutide and tirzepatide have changed the pharmacological treatment of type 2 diabetes (T2D) and obesity. Both are once-weekly subcutaneous peptides derived from the incretin hormones, yet they engage different receptors and signal differently once bound. Understanding these differences explains why tirzepatide produces greater glucose lowering and weight loss, why both drugs share most of their adverse effects, and why their benefits wane when treatment stops.

The therapeutic rationale rests on the incretin effect: oral glucose elicits far more insulin secretion than an isoglycaemic intravenous glucose infusion. In healthy people, incretin factors account for roughly 73% of the insulin response to oral glucose, but only about 36% in people with T2D [1]. Native glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) are cleared within minutes [2], so drug development focused on stable, long-acting receptor agonists. Exenatide and liraglutide established the class; semaglutide extended dosing to once weekly; and tirzepatide added a second receptor.

This review integrates molecular, physiological and clinical evidence on how each drug works. We begin with incretin biology and receptor pharmacology, then examine actions on the islet, brain, gut, adipose tissue, liver, heart and kidney, before linking mechanism to efficacy, durability and safety. We close with the unresolved GIP agonist–antagonist paradox and the next generation of incretin-based therapies. All cited references were checked against their PubMed records.

## 2. Incretin physiology

GIP is secreted by enteroendocrine K cells concentrated in the duodenum and proximal jejunum, whereas GLP-1 is secreted by L cells whose density increases toward the distal ileum and colon [2]. Both hormones act through class B G protein-coupled receptors that couple predominantly to Gαs. Receptor activation raises intracellular cAMP and engages protein kinase A and Epac2; in β cells this amplifies insulin secretion only when glucose is already elevated, which is why incretin-based drugs carry little intrinsic risk of hypoglycaemia [2].

The two receptors differ in tissue distribution and function. GLP-1R is expressed in β cells, the stomach and intestine, the heart and vasculature, the kidney and discrete brain regions; its activation stimulates insulin, suppresses glucagon, slows gastric emptying and reduces appetite [2]. GIPR is expressed in islet β and α cells, adipocytes and the brain. GIP stimulates insulin and, unlike GLP-1, promotes glucagon secretion at low glucose; in adipose tissue it enhances blood flow and triglyceride storage [3].

In T2D the two incretins diverge. Under hyperglycaemic clamp conditions, GLP-1 retained most of its insulinotropic activity and also lowered glucagon, whereas the maximal insulin response to GIP was 54% lower than in healthy controls and GIP did not lower glucagon [4]. This finding long discouraged GIP-based therapy and explains why the success of a GIP-dominant dual agonist was initially unexpected.

## 3. Molecular pharmacology

Both drugs achieve once-weekly dosing through the same two engineering steps: an α-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib) residue near the N-terminus blocks cleavage by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), and a long fatty diacid side chain binds serum albumin, slowing renal clearance. They diverge in which receptors they engage and how they signal once bound (Table 1).

### 3.1 Semaglutide

Semaglutide is an analogue of human GLP-1 with two amino acid substitutions, Aib at position 8 and arginine at position 34, and a C18 fatty diacid attached to lysine 26 through a hydrophilic linker [5]. Its GLP-1R affinity (0.38 nM) is about three-fold lower than that of liraglutide, but its albumin affinity is higher; in mini-pigs the intravenous half-life was 46 hours [5], and in humans it is approximately one week [6]. Semaglutide is a full, selective GLP-1R agonist.

### 3.2 Tirzepatide

Tirzepatide is a 39-amino-acid peptide built on the native GIP sequence, carrying Aib residues and a C20 fatty diacid moiety that supports once-weekly dosing [7]. It activates both GIPR and GLP-1R, with potency at GIPR similar to native GIP but lower potency at GLP-1R than native GLP-1 [7]. Two features distinguish it from a simple combination of the two hormones [8]:

- **Imbalanced agonism.** Receptor-occupancy modelling at clinically effective doses shows greater engagement of GIPR than of GLP-1R.
- **Biased agonism at GLP-1R.** At GIPR, tirzepatide mimics native GIP. At GLP-1R, it favours cAMP generation over β-arrestin recruitment and drives weaker receptor internalisation than GLP-1. In primary islets, β-arrestin-1 limited the insulin response to GLP-1 but not to tirzepatide, suggesting that the bias helps sustain insulinotropic signalling.

### 3.3 Structural basis

Cryogenic electron microscopy and molecular dynamics simulations of tirzepatide bound to both receptors showed that it closely resembles native GIP in how it activates GIPR, but differs markedly from GLP-1 at GLP-1R, consistent with its reduced desensitisation of that receptor [9]. The fatty acid modification, together with the peptide sequence, contributes to this mode of action [9], which illustrates that lipidation is not merely a pharmacokinetic device.

## 4. Islet actions and insulin sensitivity

Both drugs lower glucose primarily by restoring glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppressing inappropriate glucagon release. Tirzepatide adds a measurable improvement in insulin sensitivity that is only partly explained by weight loss.

In a 28-week mechanistic trial in T2D, tirzepatide 15 mg increased the clamp disposition index, an integrated measure of insulin secretion relative to sensitivity, significantly more than semaglutide 1 mg (estimated treatment difference 0.84; 95% CI 0.46–1.21) [10]. This reflected greater improvements in both total insulin secretion and clamp-derived insulin sensitivity (M value difference 1.52 mg/kg/min), and tirzepatide produced lower postprandial glucose, insulin and glucagon concentrations than semaglutide on meal testing [10].

Preclinical work supports a direct GIPR contribution to insulin sensitisation. In obese mice lacking GLP-1R, and therefore without GLP-1R-driven weight loss, tirzepatide still improved insulin sensitivity by enhancing glucose disposal in white adipose tissue; a long-acting selective GIPR agonist reproduced this effect, which was accompanied by lower circulating branched-chain amino acids and ketoacids [11]. At the islet, the GIP component may also preserve counter-regulatory glucagon release as glucose falls [3].

## 5. Central nervous system actions

Weight loss with both drugs comes predominantly from eating less. In adults with obesity, 20 weeks of semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced ad libitum energy intake by 35% compared with placebo, with less hunger, greater fullness, better control of eating and fewer and weaker food cravings [12]. In rodents, semaglutide reduced food intake and altered food preference without decreasing energy expenditure [13].

### 5.1 Access to the brain

Albumin-bound semaglutide does not cross the intact blood–brain barrier. Fluorescently labelled semaglutide reached the brain through circumventricular organs and selected periventricular sites, including the brainstem, septal nucleus and hypothalamus [13]. From these entry points it activated neurons in ten brain areas, including secondary regions without direct GLP-1R engagement such as the lateral parabrachial nucleus, a node implicated in meal termination [13].

### 5.2 Hypothalamus

In the arcuate nucleus, labelled liraglutide was internalised by pro-opiomelanocortin/cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (POMC/CART) neurons. GLP-1 directly excited these anorexigenic neurons and indirectly inhibited orexigenic neuropeptide Y/agouti-related peptide (NPY/AgRP) neurons through GABA-dependent signalling [14]. Weight loss in rats persisted when GLP-1R was removed from vagal afferents, the area postrema or the paraventricular nucleus, pointing to arcuate POMC/CART neurons as a likely key site [14].

### 5.3 Hindbrain and reward

The area postrema (AP) and nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) integrate vagal and circulating signals. Semaglutide increased expression of prolactin-releasing hormone and tyrosine hydroxylase in the AP [13]. Hindbrain GLP-1R activation promotes satiation but also mediates nausea and conditioned aversion, linking efficacy and the main tolerability limit of the class. Beyond homeostatic feeding, GLP-1R signalling dampens reward. In a phase 2 trial in 48 adults with alcohol use disorder, low-dose semaglutide reduced laboratory alcohol self-administration, drinks per drinking day and craving [15].

### 5.4 Central GIPR signalling

Three lines of evidence suggest that the brain contributes to the added efficacy of tirzepatide. First, mice lacking GIPR in the central nervous system were resistant to the weight-lowering effect of acylated GIP, and the superiority of GIP/GLP-1 co-agonism over GLP-1 agonism alone was lost [16]. Second, GIPR is expressed on distinct inhibitory (GABAergic) and excitatory neuron populations in the AP/NTS; in mice, rats and musk shrews, GIPR agonism blocked emesis and illness behaviours induced by GLP-1R agonists while preserving reduced food intake and weight loss [17]. Third, the picture is heterogeneous: hypothalamic GIPR was not required for the synergy of co-agonism on body weight, and chemogenetic activation of dorsal vagal complex GIPR neurons reduced intake but also induced conditioned taste avoidance [18]. Peripherally administered GIPR agonists reached only circumventricular organs [18].

Together, these data support two complementary hypotheses: GIPR adds anorexigenic signalling, and GIPR improves tolerability, allowing greater effective GLP-1R engagement. Which predominates in humans is unresolved.

## 6. Gastrointestinal actions

GLP-1R activation slows gastric emptying through vagal pathways, flattening postprandial glucose excursions [2]. With long-acting agonists this effect is largely lost over time. After 20 weeks of semaglutide 2.4 mg, gastric emptying assessed by paracetamol absorption showed no evidence of delay: early absorption measures were unchanged, and a small difference in total absorption over 5 hours was not significant after adjustment for body weight [12]. Gastric slowing is therefore unlikely to explain sustained weight loss, which was 9.9% in the same study [12].

The same vagal and hindbrain pathways underlie the dominant adverse effects of the class: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation, which cluster during dose escalation (Section 10).

## 7. Adipose, liver, cardiovascular and renal effects

### 7.1 Adipose tissue

GLP-1R expression in adipocytes is sparse, whereas GIPR is abundant. GIP increases adipose blood flow and promotes lipoprotein lipase-mediated triglyceride uptake. The lipid-buffering hypothesis proposes that improved subcutaneous storage reduces spill-over of lipid into liver and muscle, improving insulin sensitivity [3]. The weight-independent insulin sensitisation seen in GLP-1R-null mice supports this model [11].

### 7.2 Liver

Hepatocytes express little or no GLP-1R, so liver benefits are thought to be largely indirect, reflecting weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity and reduced lipotoxicity. In the phase 3 ESSENCE trial in biopsy-proven metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with stage F2–F3 fibrosis, semaglutide 2.4 mg achieved resolution of steatohepatitis in 62.9% of patients versus 34.3% with placebo, and fibrosis improvement in 36.8% versus 22.4%, at 72 weeks [19]. In the phase 2 SYNERGY-NASH trial, tirzepatide resolved MASH in 44–62% of participants across doses versus 10% with placebo at 52 weeks [20].

### 7.3 Heart and vasculature

GLP-1R is expressed in the sinoatrial node, vascular smooth muscle and immune cells. Agonism lowers blood pressure, increases resting heart rate and reduces systemic inflammation [2]. In SUSTAIN-6, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in T2D (hazard ratio [HR] 0.74; 95% CI 0.58–0.95) [6]. In SELECT, which enrolled 17,604 people with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity but without diabetes, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced MACE by 20% (HR 0.80; 95% CI 0.72–0.90) [21].

Weight loss does not fully explain this benefit. In a prespecified SELECT analysis, MACE reduction was consistent across all categories of baseline weight and waist circumference; weight loss at week 20 showed no linear relationship with subsequent MACE risk; and an estimated 33% of the benefit was statistically mediated by reduction in waist circumference [22]. The remainder is attributed to direct vascular, anti-inflammatory and haemodynamic effects. In heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and obesity, semaglutide improved symptoms and physical limitation and reduced C-reactive protein by 43.5% versus 7.3% with placebo [23], while tirzepatide reduced the composite of cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure (HR 0.62; 95% CI 0.41–0.95) [24]. In SURPASS-CVOT, tirzepatide was non-inferior but not superior to dulaglutide for MACE (HR 0.92; 95.3% CI 0.83–1.01) [25].

### 7.4 Kidney

In FLOW, semaglutide 1 mg reduced major kidney disease events by 24% in people with T2D and chronic kidney disease (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.66–0.88), slowed the annual decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) by 1.16 mL/min/1.73 m², and reduced cardiovascular death (HR 0.71) and death from any cause (HR 0.80) [26]. Proposed mechanisms include reduced glomerular hyperfiltration, natriuresis, lower blood pressure and anti-inflammatory effects.

## 8. The GIP paradox

A central unresolved question is why both activating and blocking GIPR, when combined with GLP-1R agonism, enhance weight loss. Genome-wide association studies implicate GIPR in obesity, and GIPR knockout mice resist diet-induced obesity [27]. Antagonistic anti-GIPR antibodies protected obese mice and non-human primates against weight gain, and weight loss was enhanced when they were co-administered with GLP-1R agonists [27]. Maridebart cafraglutide (AMG 133), a GIPR-antagonist antibody conjugated to two GLP-1 analogue peptides, produced dose-dependent weight loss in a phase 1 trial that was maintained for up to 150 days after the last dose [28].

Several hypotheses attempt to reconcile these observations:

- **Receptor desensitisation.** Chronic GIPR agonism desensitised GIPR in primary adipocytes in vitro and in adipose tissue in vivo, so a long-acting agonist functioned like an antagonist in that tissue [29].
- **Tissue-specific effects.** Agonism may dominate in the central nervous system, adding anorexia and suppressing emesis [16,17], while antagonist-like effects dominate in adipose tissue.
- **Different routes to the same end.** Agonism may act chiefly through tolerability and adipose insulin sensitisation, and antagonism chiefly by amplifying GLP-1R-driven anorexia.

Direct mechanistic comparisons in humans are needed. Because tirzepatide is GIPR-favoured [8], its clinical profile cannot be read simply as GLP-1R agonism with a small GIP contribution.

## 9. Clinical efficacy linked to mechanism

In T2D, SURPASS-2 compared tirzepatide 5, 10 and 15 mg with semaglutide 1 mg over 40 weeks in 1,879 patients: HbA1c fell by 2.01, 2.24 and 2.30 percentage points with tirzepatide versus 1.86 points with semaglutide, and weight fell 1.9–5.5 kg more with tirzepatide [30]. In obesity without diabetes, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced weight by 14.9% versus 2.4% with placebo at 68 weeks (STEP 1) [31], and tirzepatide 15 mg by 20.9% versus 3.1% at 72 weeks (SURMOUNT-1) [32]. In the head-to-head SURMOUNT-5 trial, tirzepatide reduced weight by 20.2% versus 13.7% with semaglutide at 72 weeks, with greater waist reduction (18.4 vs 13.0 cm) [33] (Figure 2).

Three mechanistic explanations for tirzepatide's advantage are compatible with these data: additional central anorexigenic signalling via GIPR [16]; better tolerability through hindbrain GIPR, allowing greater effective GLP-1R engagement [17]; and GIPR-mediated adipose insulin sensitisation [10,11]. Cross-trial comparisons are limited by differences in populations, dose escalation and estimands, and SURMOUNT-5 was open-label.

### 9.1 Durability and the chronic nature of the mechanism

Because these drugs act on homeostatic and reward circuits rather than permanently altering them, their effects depend on continued exposure. One year after stopping semaglutide and lifestyle intervention in the STEP 1 extension, participants regained two-thirds of their prior weight loss, and cardiometabolic improvements reverted toward baseline [37]. In SURMOUNT-4, after a 36-week tirzepatide lead-in producing 20.9% weight loss, participants switched to placebo regained 14.0% of body weight over 52 weeks, whereas those who continued tirzepatide lost a further 5.5% [38].

## 10. Safety and mechanism-linked adverse effects

Most adverse effects of both drugs are predictable extensions of incretin receptor pharmacology (Table 3). Gastrointestinal events dominate: in SURPASS-2, nausea occurred in 17–22% of tirzepatide recipients and 18% of semaglutide recipients [30]. Discontinuation because of adverse events in SURMOUNT-1 was 4.3–7.1% with tirzepatide versus 2.6% with placebo [32], and in STEP 1, 4.5% of semaglutide recipients stopped because of gastrointestinal events versus 0.8% on placebo [31].

Lean mass is a growing concern. A network meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials found that lean mass accounted for about 25% of weight lost with GLP-1R-based agents; tirzepatide 15 mg and semaglutide 2.4 mg were the most effective at reducing fat but among the least lean-sparing [39]. This has prompted trials combining incretin drugs with inhibitors of myostatin and activin signalling, such as bimagrumab [40].

In rodents, GLP-1R agonists activate thyroid C cells, causing calcitonin release and C-cell hyperplasia. Primate C cells express little GLP-1R and did not respond, and 20 months of high-dose liraglutide did not cause C-cell hyperplasia in monkeys [41]. Human relevance is therefore uncertain, but both drugs are contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2. Rapid glycaemic improvement was associated with more diabetic retinopathy complications in SUSTAIN-6 (HR 1.76; 95% CI 1.11–2.78) [6]. In a single-centre retrospective cohort, semaglutide was associated with a higher 36-month cumulative incidence of non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION) in patients with T2D (8.9% vs 1.8%) [42]; causality and mechanism remain unresolved.

## 11. Knowledge gaps and future directions

The field is moving from one or two receptors to several, and from injected peptides to oral small molecules. Retatrutide, which adds glucagon receptor agonism to GIPR and GLP-1R agonism, reduced weight by 24.2% at 48 weeks with the 12 mg dose in phase 2 [36]. CagriSema, which combines semaglutide with the long-acting amylin analogue cagrilintide, reduced weight by 20.4% versus 3.0% with placebo at 68 weeks in REDEFINE 1 [35]. Orforglipron, a once-daily oral non-peptide GLP-1R agonist, reduced weight by 11.2% with the 36 mg dose versus 2.1% with placebo at 72 weeks [34]. Further clinical trials of GIPR antagonism with maridebart cafraglutide will directly test the GIP paradox [28].

Key mechanistic questions remain open:

- Which tissues mediate the GIPR contribution in humans, and does sustained agonism act as functional antagonism?
- How much of the cardiovascular and renal benefit is independent of weight loss, and is it shared by tirzepatide?
- Can lean mass be preserved without sacrificing fat loss?
- How can benefit be maintained after treatment is reduced or stopped?
- What is the long-term safety of chronic multi-receptor agonism, including ocular and pancreatobiliary effects?

## 12. Conclusions

Semaglutide acts through potent, sustained and selective GLP-1R activation across the islet, gut, brain, heart and kidney. Tirzepatide engages the same pathways through a GIPR-favoured, cAMP-biased dual agonist profile that adds central GIPR signalling, potential tolerability benefits and adipose-mediated insulin sensitisation. These differences are consistent with tirzepatide's greater glycaemic and weight effects, whereas semaglutide currently has the broader base of proven cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. For both drugs, the dominant mechanism of weight loss is central suppression of food intake, which is why benefits persist only with continued treatment. Resolving the GIP paradox and the weight-independent mechanisms of organ protection will shape the next generation of incretin-based medicines.
